## Overview

Integrated Digital Delivery (IDD) is one of the key thrusts of Singapore's Built Environment Industry Transformation Map (BEITM). BCA describes it as a holistic approach that uses digital technologies to connect all stakeholders involved in a construction project, streamlining and simplifying every stage of a building's lifecycle — from design to fabrication, construction and asset management.

The concept is broader than adopting individual software tools. IDD is about connecting project parties and information flows across the whole delivery process, so that design data, fabrication data, construction data and asset data remain useful beyond the stage in which they were created.

## The IDD framework

BCA presents IDD as a four-stage lifecycle:

1. **Design**
2. **Fabrication**
3. **Construction**
4. **Asset management**

The framework is supported by materials on the benefits of IDD for projects and stakeholders, including a video case for adoption. The underlying logic is that digital continuity across these stages reduces rework, improves coordination and allows information created during design and construction to be handed over for operations.

## IDD use cases

BCA has identified several IDD use cases across the design, fabrication, construction and asset management stages. Of these, it highlights **19 IDD essential use cases**, published as a separate PDF.

The essential use cases are intended to drive greater adoption of IDD and collaboration among project stakeholders, and to demonstrate the benefits of adopting IDD for streamlining construction project stages. They are not simply a technology catalogue; they define practical activities that project teams can specify, deliver and measure.

## Getting started with IDD implementation

BCA provides two principal guides for organisations that are new to IDD or want to improve current practice.

### Strategic IDD Adoption Guide

The Strategic IDD Adoption Guide covers:

- IDD fundamentals
- key benefits
- planning considerations
- use cases
- recommended KPI indicators

It is aimed at organisations that need to build a case and a plan for adoption, rather than only a list of technologies.

### IDD Requirements Guide

The IDD Requirements Guide helps clients articulate IDD requirements in tender documents. It covers three essential aspects:

1. **Use cases and technologies**
2. **Roles and responsibilities**
3. **Key deliverables**

This is significant because IDD requirements have to be procured, not assumed. If a client does not specify the use cases, the responsibilities of each party and the deliverables it expects, the digital workflow can easily revert to disconnected files and informal coordination.

### Shared Services Provider programme

Organisations that need IDD professional services can refer to the Shared Services Provider (SSP) Programme administered by the Specialists Trade Alliance of Singapore (STAS), which maintains a list of registered providers.

## Foundations: BIM, data and digital competency

BCA frames IDD implementation as depending on three foundations.

### Quality BIM models

Project teams need quality Building Information Modelling (BIM) models with clear specifications, structured data and standardised processes. These support accurate and efficient project delivery. IDD therefore depends on BIM model quality, not just on the existence of models.

### Robust data management

The standards referenced by BCA provide a foundation for structured data exchange and informed decision-making. IDD requires data to remain usable across parties and stages, which in turn depends on common standards, structured information and agreed exchange practices.

### Digital competency

As the built environment sector transforms and digitalises, firms need strong digital competencies to stay competitive. BCA points to the Digital Delivery Management accreditation as a way to raise these competencies across the workforce. This connects IDD to the workforce and capability agenda rather than treating it as a purely technical programme.

## What IDD is trying to change

The IDD page makes a small number of claims with large implications:

- **Digital continuity across the lifecycle.** IDD connects design, fabrication, construction and asset management rather than optimising each stage in isolation.
- **Collaboration across stakeholders.** The approach is explicitly about connecting all stakeholders involved in a construction project.
- **Use cases before tools.** BCA's guidance is organised around essential use cases, adoption planning and project requirements, not around a single product.
- **Procurement is a lever.** The IDD Requirements Guide is designed to help clients write IDD requirements into tender documents, covering use cases, roles and deliverables.
- **Foundations matter.** Quality BIM models, structured data exchange and digital competency are treated as prerequisites for successful IDD.

## Hidden costs and workflow change

NUS-ISS adds a practitioner's caution to the policy case. Mr Chua Kiong Kiat describes IDD as using digital technologies to integrate work processes and connect stakeholders throughout the construction and building life cycle — covering design, fabrication and assembly on site, and the operations and maintenance of buildings. He accepts the benefits, from operational efficiency to worker safety, but argues it is "also crucial to acknowledge the hidden costs and unique challenges that come with it."

Those costs fall into three areas:

- **Investment beyond technology.** Embracing BIM and AI requires spending on training and infrastructure, not just software, plus "a fundamental shift in mindset."
- **Workflow overhaul.** Most planning and iteration-related workflows shift into the earlier design phase. The claimed payoff is lower safety risk and fewer costly fixes, because potential issues are resolved in the model before construction.
- **Siloed data models.** Different parties use different digital models, and for the same project the information needed by architects differs from that needed by structural engineers. Collaboration across stakeholders is a requirement, not a by-product of buying the same software.

Chua also warns against reading too much into technology demonstrations. Publicity about industrial robots that assemble brick walls does not reflect the complexity contractors face on real construction sites, because controlled trial environments are not representative.

This is consistent with BCA's emphasis on foundations. IDD is not a procurement exercise in which a client buys a platform; it changes when work is done, who is responsible for which information, and how much planning has to happen before construction starts.
