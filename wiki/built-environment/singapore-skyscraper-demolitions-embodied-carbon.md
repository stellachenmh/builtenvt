# Singapore's Skyscraper Demolitions and Embodied Carbon

> Sources: Teo Kai Xiang, The Straits Times, 2024-11-09
> Raw: [Sky-high demolitions: What is behind Singapore's many skyscraper tear-downs](../../raw/built-environment/2024-11-09-singapore-skyscraper-demolitions.md)
> Updated: 2026-11-10

## Overview

Singapore is home to 10 of the world's tallest skyscrapers ever demolished as at 2024, second only to New York City, according to the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat. The buildings torn down and rebuilt were no older than 45 years, and the trend is driven by limited land, plot-ratio intensification, the Urban Redevelopment Authority's CBD incentive scheme, changing market needs and redevelopment profit.

The environmental cost is the less visible side of that cycle. The built environment is responsible for 39 per cent of global energy-related carbon emissions, and while operational carbon normally accounts for nearly 70 per cent of a building's emissions against 30 per cent embodied, shorter lifespans can push embodied carbon to up to 40 per cent. That leaves roughly 60 per cent operational — a shift that turns demolition timing into a carbon decision, not only a heritage one.

## The scale of demolition

The 2024 ranking lists the 100 tallest buildings demolished in the world. Singapore's 10 entries put it ahead of Tokyo with eight, with Hong Kong and Sydney in fourth and fifth place respectively. New York City leads with 25.

The former AXA Tower in Shenton Way holds the record for the tallest skyscraper ever demolished, after New York's World Trade Center. It was 235m tall and was taken down in 2023.

## Why buildings come down

Experts attribute the pace to Singapore's land constraints and the shifting needs of its residents. Mr Mok Wei Wei, managing director at W Architects, describes the attitude to buildings without historic or heritage value:

> "This has always been the kind of attitude to buildings that are not classified as historic or of heritage value."

He points to the plot-ratio revisions of the 1990s and 2000s, when condominiums less than 20 years old were taken down because "there was great money to be made in pulling down these buildings and rebuilding them".

Dr Joseph Ooi, professor of real estate at the National University of Singapore, frames demolition and redevelopment as part of the life cycle of a city that must accommodate economic progress. With limited greenfield land, Singapore intensifies existing sites by raising plot ratios, which creates the opportunity for increased developer profits.

Developers cite increased floor area, improved sustainability features and demand for mixed-use developments. The article's examples show the pattern:

| Building | Fate | Redevelopment outcome |
|----------|------|-----------------------|
| Fuji Xerox Towers | Demolished by City Developments Limited in 2023 | Newport Plaza, a 25 per cent increase in floor area to about 655,000 sq ft |
| AXA Tower | Demolished in 2023 | A 305m building due in 2028, with an over 50 per cent increase in total floor space |
| Former CPF Building | Demolished in 2017 | CapitaSky, with a nearly 25 per cent increase in total gross area to over 600,500 sq ft |
| UIC Building | Demolished in 2013 | A new building with 60 per cent more floor space, including V on Shenton |
| Pearl Bank Apartments | Demolished in 2019 | A new luxury development with an equivalent floor space and plot ratio |
| Shaw Tower | Being redeveloped | 60m taller, with 10 per cent more floor space |

Pearl Bank is the exception that tests the profit explanation: it was built in 1976 and demolished in 2019 for a development with an equivalent floor space and plot ratio. Mr Mok says smaller units were built to maximise profits.

A Skywaters penthouse in the AXA Tower replacement was among the most expensive luxury residences sold in the first half of 2024, at $47.3 million or $6,100 per square foot.

## Policy as a demolition driver

Dr Lee Kwan Ok, associate professor and deputy head of the NUS department of real estate, links many CBD demolitions to the URA's CBD incentive scheme. Introduced in 2019, the scheme offers developers increased plot ratios to rejuvenate the CBD into a more mixed-use precinct with more housing and amenities.

She supports the mixed-use push but criticises the eligibility criterion that a building need only be over 20 years old: "When you think of high-rises made with good materials, they can last 100 years," she says.

Her recommendations are to raise the minimum age for redevelopment under the scheme, further incentivise adaptive reuse and adopt formal heritage impact assessments, a practice common in Hong Kong but neither mandatory nor widely conducted in Singapore.

## Carbon accounting: operational versus embodied

Associate Professor Yeo Kang Shua of the Singapore University of Technology and Design argues that environmental sustainability cannot be assessed on operational carbon alone:

> "For a more holistic view, embodied carbon must be included in the equation together with operational carbon when discussing environmental sustainability."

Embodied carbon covers a building's full life cycle — materials, transport to site, assembly, demolition and disposal. Operational carbon is the ongoing footprint from day-to-day energy use.

Ms Ruchi Bhatia, executive director of energy and sustainability at Meinhardt Group, gives the shares: operational carbon accounts for nearly 70 per cent of a building's carbon emissions and embodied carbon 30 per cent. With constant urban redevelopment and renewal shortening lifespans, she says research shows embodied carbon could account for up to 40 per cent of total emissions. The implied operational share in that case is about 60 per cent.

The policy response includes the Singapore Building Carbon Calculator, jointly developed by the Building and Construction Authority (BCA), the Singapore Green Building Council and other groups to quantify and plan carbon reduction from early design. BCA also points to Green Mark Platinum awards and to CapitaSky, redeveloped from the former CPF Building, as a sustainable redevelopment example: it was the first redevelopment commercial project in Singapore's CBD to achieve 100 per cent reuse of existing bored piles, saving 8,400 tonnes of concrete and contributing to a 37 per cent reduction in carbon emissions.

Bhatia cautions against treating preservation as automatically greener. Refurbished buildings may have poorer operational emissions, while new buildings' sustainability features may not offset the impact of demolition.

## Heritage and urban identity

The conservation framework took shape in the 1980s. The Conservation Master Plan was unveiled in 1986, and conservation areas were created in Kampong Glam and Emerald Hill in 1989. Mr Mok argues that the same care is rarely applied to modern, nondescript buildings.

The losses include Comcentre, built in 1979 and designed by Alfred Wong, which served as Singtel's headquarters for 45 years; the former CPF Building; the UIC Building, once Singapore's tallest for a year in 1973; and Pearl Bank Apartments. Golden Mile Complex, gazetted for conservation in 2021, was the first building of its kind to receive that status, since most conserved buildings date from the colonial era before the 1960s.

Dr Lee warns that rapid redevelopment can cost the city its urban identity as well as carbon: "If we say that sustainability is our national agenda, then isn't this a contradiction?"

A URA spokesman says the agency takes a selective approach, proposing significant buildings for conservation and working with stakeholders on sensitive restoration and integration. The former Police Academy site in Mount Pleasant is cited as an example where some old buildings will be retained. The spokesman adds that land constraints make it impossible to conserve every building.

Views differ on what is worth keeping. Mr Mok says buildings need time before their architectural merits can be assessed, and points to old Toa Payoh HDB blocks now being looked at for conservation. Dr Ooi takes a building-by-building view: in an era that produced many skyscrapers, tearing down 10 still leaves a lot from that era. He acknowledges that heritage, culture and history give people identity and memory, but says those values are hard to quantify.

Developers also use memory as a design device. CapitaLand's replacement for Pearl Bank pays homage to the horseshoe shape with twin C-shaped blocks and roof bridges, while Shaw Towers Realty commissioned a study on how the 1975 Shaw Tower could be represented in its redevelopment, including facade panels, the Shaw Brothers logo, auditorium seats, ticket stubs and film memorabilia.

## Adaptive reuse as the alternative

Adaptive reuse — retrofitting or adapting older buildings for new purposes — is presented as the main alternative to demolition.

The article's financial precedents are substantial. City Developments Limited spent $70 million retrofitting Republic Plaza in Raffles Place in 2018 instead of tearing it down; at 280m, it is the second-tallest building in Singapore. Singapore Land Group's $160 million renovation of Singapore Land Tower took three years, was expected to be completed by the end of 2024, and resulted in savings of more than 50 per cent of embodied carbon emissions.

Mr Mok argues that a building with "good bones" can be adapted, and that older buildings carry a character people find attractive, from shophouses to pre-2000s high-rises and HDB flats. Adaptive reuse can also be more cost-effective for smaller enterprises than new builds or shopping centres.

The architect of Pearl Bank Apartments, Tan Cheng Siong, made the case before its demolition in 2018:

> "The redevelopment formula does not have to comprise only tearing down a structure, especially if the original building has high architectural and heritage value."

Bhatia's conclusion is that a balanced approach is necessary. Redevelopment should be judged on whole-life carbon and on the socio-economic benefits of denser, mixed-use "live-work-play-grow-commute-less" models, not on a single metric.

## Key takeaways

- **Demolition is a carbon decision.** Moving a building's end of life forward shifts emissions from operational to embodied carbon, and the article's figures imply the operational share can fall from nearly 70 per cent to about 60 per cent when embodied carbon rises to 40 per cent.
- **Short lifespans are the mechanism.** Singapore's demolished skyscrapers were no older than 45 years, and the average is driven down further by redevelopment for floor area, plot ratio and profit.
- **Policy shapes the cycle.** Plot-ratio revisions and the URA's CBD incentive scheme, with its over-20-year eligibility threshold, set the terms on which redevelopment becomes attractive.
- **Redevelopment is not automatically worse.** New buildings can operate more efficiently and deliver socio-economic benefits, but those gains have to be weighed against the embodied carbon written off in demolition.
- **Adaptive reuse has working precedents.** Republic Plaza and Singapore Land Tower show that retrofits can preserve embodied carbon at scale, while heritage retention addresses the urban-identity loss the article documents.
