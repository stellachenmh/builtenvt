# Smart Facilities Management: Integrated, Aggregated and Smart FM

> Sources: Building and Construction Authority (BCA), *Guide to Smart FM*, version 2.2, 31 July 2026
> Raw: [Guide to Smart FM (v2.2, 31 July 2026)](../../raw/built-environment/2026-07-31-guide-to-smart-fm.md)
> Updated: 2026-11-10

## Overview

Singapore's *Guide to Smart FM* defines Smart FM as "the integration of systems, processes, technologies, and personnel to enhance the management of a building's facilities". It is a means to an end, not a technology programme: the Guide stresses that Smart FM "should not begin with technology in mind", and that process and people matter as much as the systems deployed.

The Guide was developed by the Integrated Smart FM Taskforce under the Facilities Management Implementation Committee (FMIC) and launched on 1 October 2019. The FMIC itself was formed in April 2018, after the Real Estate Industry Transformation Map was launched in February 2018, to drive adoption of smart FM technology and streamline FM work processes. Version 2.2, dated 31 July 2026, adds guidance on Integrated FM and Aggregated FM (IFM and AFM), Type 3 Smart FM, data management, digital skills and new case studies.

The framing problem is structural. Singapore faces an ageing workforce and an ageing building stock, while FM has traditionally been labour-intensive and reactive. Smart FM is positioned as the route from manual inspection and fixed maintenance schedules to predictive maintenance, data analytics and outcome-based service delivery. It also connects FM transformation to sustainability goals under the Singapore Green Plan 2030, because predictive maintenance and energy optimisation often draw on the same building data.

## Integration and aggregation

The Guide treats integration and aggregation as "two sides of the same Smart FM coin":

- **Integration** refers to bundling two or more interdisciplinary services — for example cleaning, security and mechanical and electrical (M&E) services — together.
- **Aggregation** refers to consolidating demand and resources, such as across a portfolio of buildings or at a district level.

Integration is not the same as single sourcing, multi-tasking or a loose consortium of companies bidding for a project. The Guide states: "Integration is not equivalent to single sourcing, multi-tasking nor a loose consortium of companies to bid for a project." A genuinely integrated FM model delivers the full spectrum of FM services, coordinated through a single platform.

Integration can occur at three points in the delivery model:

1. **At service delivery**, through multi-functional robots or multi-skilled personnel.
2. **At response**, where subsystems are linked to complement each other, such as robot integration with lifts.
3. **At command level**, through workforce management tools that give the FM company visibility of personnel, equipment and workflow for optimal deployment and outcome reporting.

Aggregation can happen on the **demand side**, where a building owner with a portfolio consolidates demand for a better overview and management of resources, or on the **supply side**, where FM companies and service providers manage several contracts as a pool. The Guide argues that both are intrinsic to outcome-based contracts: they give FM companies the latitude to exploit their competitive edge and manage resources optimally while meeting each building owner's needs.

The Guide applies a process, technology and people lens to this change:

- **Process:** outcome-based integrated FM contracts turn the relationship between building owners, FM companies and service providers into a longer-term partnership.
- **Technology:** solutions should be reviewed against business requirements and existing FM processes, then replicated or scaled to monitor multiple buildings and support predictive and prescriptive maintenance.
- **People:** regular training, upskilling and cross-training allow staff to take on broader, multi-skilled roles across FM disciplines and facilities.

## Three types of "smartness"

The Smart FM Framework classifies solutions into three types. The Guide is explicit that these are **not an order of merit or maturity**. The choice should be driven by a cost-benefit analysis against the desired outcomes.

| Type | Description | What it does | Example from the Guide |
|------|-------------|--------------|------------------------|
| **Type 1** | Digitalised workflow automation | When triggered by an incident, automatically initiates a process that tracks, logs and closes the incident | Remote lift monitoring; automatic breakdown detection and technician dispatch |
| **Type 2** | Optimisation within systems | Uses data analytics to optimise systems, quantify FM efficiency, perform preventive or predictive maintenance and intervene before serious issues develop | Gathering data from multiple connected units to improve diagnostics |
| **Type 3** | Integration across systems | Optimises resource deployment and utilisation across many systems | Integrating a lift vendor's API with the building management system |

The distinction matters commercially. A Type 1 solution can digitise a form or a work order without changing how systems talk to each other. A Type 2 solution can optimise a chiller plant or a lift fleet using data from that system. A Type 3 solution connects systems so that data and decisions cross operational boundaries — which is the technical foundation for IFM and AFM.

The Guide's Schindler Ahead case shows the three types inside one product. Schindler Ahead includes Type 1 digitalised workflow automation, Type 2 optimisation within systems and Type 3 integration across systems. At the customer in the case, the current model was Model B: Schindler Ahead was procured as a standalone service with no integration to other Smart FM systems. Schindler's long-term goal was Model A, through an open, secure and modular integration toolbox with APIs. The case reports that technical callbacks per month fell by 43% and total downtime per month fell by 94% after installing the monitoring cubes in lifts.

## The 5-step SMART process

The Guide recommends a five-step process for building owners and FM managers. It is iterative: Step 5 feeds back into Steps 1 to 4.

| Step | Purpose | Core question |
|------|---------|---------------|
| **1. Set business objectives and outcomes** | Align Smart FM with business goals, desired FM outcomes, KPIs and priority services | What am I trying to achieve, and which FM services matter most? |
| **2. Map out Smart FM solutions as enablers** | Build a Smart FM Framework, classify solutions by type and identify aggregation opportunities | Which type of "smartness" does each priority service need? |
| **3. Adopt a suitable implementation model** | Choose Model A, Model B, a hybrid or a phased approach, and specify system requirements | How will the solutions be implemented and integrated? |
| **4. Review the procurement contract** | Move to outcome-based measurements, longer terms and appropriate technical specifications | What contract will make the desired outcomes achievable? |
| **5. Track outcomes and review for continuous improvement** | Evaluate results against the original objectives and revisit the earlier steps | Did the solutions deliver, and what should change? |

### Step 1: Set business objectives and outcomes

Step 1 starts from the fundamentals of integrating people, place, process and technology. The Guide ties Smart FM to three corporate pillars: delivering efficient FM operations, ensuring occupant satisfaction, and enhancing building assets through upgrading or proper maintenance.

The practical tool is a template that ranks business objectives, sets desired FM outcomes and KPIs, and identifies high-priority FM services. Example outcomes in the Guide include reducing operating cost, increasing user satisfaction and reducing complaints, and raising internal FM staff productivity despite a headcount freeze and an ageing workforce.

### Step 2: Map out Smart FM solutions as enablers

Step 2 builds a Smart FM Framework that maps solutions to FM services and desired outcomes. It also classifies each solution by Type 1, 2 or 3, and asks whether aggregation is feasible across a cluster of buildings or a portfolio.

The Guide encourages building owners to think beyond operations. Data collected from people-traffic information, for example, could help a mall time promotions through its customer app or help cleaning teams match manpower to peak and non-peak periods.

### Step 3: Adopt a suitable implementation model

There are two reference models:

| Model | Description | Trade-offs |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **Model A: Integrated Smart FM Solution** | Smart solutions feed data into an integrated software suite, either provided by the appointed FM company or acquired and operated by the building owner | The FM-provided route is resource-light and gives a reasonably comprehensive dashboard; the owner-acquired route requires more scale and resources but allows customisation and suits large portfolios |
| **Model B: Single Smart FM Solution** | The owner procures specific Smart FM services and solutions individually, each managed through its own provider's tools | Leverages specialist expertise and data, but creates integration risk unless the solutions have open APIs |

The Guide is careful not to rank the models: "none of the implementation models are superior to another." The choice depends on business objectives, operational priorities and desired FM outcomes. A phased adoption, variation or hybrid is entirely possible. A large portfolio might start with a centralised FM operations centre under Model A, then subscribe to a chiller vendor's remote monitoring service under Model B to access the vendor's proprietary knowledge and global installed base.

Model A examples in the Guide include JTC Corporation's J-Ops and CapitaLand's Integrated Building Platform. The Guide also notes that specialised suppliers — major chiller suppliers, for example — have developed connected services for remote monitoring and predictive maintenance.

Whichever model is chosen, system and technical requirements should be written into procurement contracts. These include the data and format fed to the integrated FM system, the "north-bound" interface through which subsystems lodge incidents, and the fields needed to compute outcome-based KPIs, such as incidents per week, mean time to failure, energy efficiency index, energy savings, financial savings, turnaround time or time to resolution.

### Step 4: Review the procurement contract

The Guide describes a shift from traditional headcount-based procurement to outcome-based procurement. This allows less resource-intensive solutions to enter the market and encourages FM companies to improve operations to meet building owners' expectations.

Longer contracts are central to the model. The Guide recommends a contract duration of "at least three years, with another three years extension". In some public agencies that have adopted Smart FM, contract durations range "between five to ten years". Longer terms give service and solution providers more certainty to invest in technology and upgrade building infrastructure, and allow employees to upskill and take on more complex roles.

Outcome-based measurement requires different specifications for different stakeholders:

- **Financial stakeholders** want to know the impact on financial metrics and the interface to accounting systems.
- **Operational stakeholders** care about occupant satisfaction, ease of operation, access to critical system information and maintenance productivity.
- **Sustainability stakeholders** care about energy and water efficiency, utility optimisation and carbon reduction.
- **Productivity stakeholders** care about occupant comfort, space utilisation and workflow productivity.
- **Security stakeholders** may want access control, emergency messaging and people-location capabilities.
- **Public communications stakeholders** may want performance data in lobby displays to promote initiatives and help visitors.

The Guide also introduces the Integrated Facilities Management (IFM) contract as a way to aggregate demand within a building or across a portfolio. Combined with a longer outcome-based term, IFM gives providers payback certainty and flexibility to deploy IoT, integrated command centres, digital twin modelling and interoperable FM solutions.

### Step 5: Track outcomes and review for continuous improvement

Step 5 evaluates whether the adopted solutions met the business objectives and desired FM outcomes. The Guide provides a review table that checks each objective against its KPIs, assesses whether the solutions were effective, records what went well or wrong, and identifies improvements.

The review then loops back:

- **Step 1:** Have business objectives, outcomes or service priorities changed?
- **Step 2:** Are there new or emerging solutions that better meet the need?
- **Step 3:** Should a different implementation model be adopted, or should system requirements be enhanced?
- **Step 4:** Should outcome-based measurements be redefined to improve service quality?

## Digitalisation Maturity Map

The Guide observes that many FM stakeholders have adopted Smart FM technology, but that system-to-system integration has progressed minimally. The Digitalisation Maturity Map is intended to align digital adoption across the FM value chain so that data can be shared and synergy captured.

It defines three categories of stakeholder:

| Category | Stakeholder | Stages |
|----------|-------------|--------|
| **Category 1** | Digitally ready vendors | 1.1 Sense and capture; 1.2 Adaptive actions; 1.3 Resource optimisation |
| **Category 2** | Digitally ready FM companies | 2.1 Integrated service management; 2.2 Intelligent insights |
| **Category 3** | Digitally ready building owners | 3.1 Integration and aggregation |

The map is applied trade by trade. For cleaning, smart bins, ammonia sensors and soap-dispenser sensors generate alerts; the vendor designs response workflows and uses historical data to optimise cleaning frequency and scheduling; the FM company consolidates cleaning data with other trades; the owner uses the resulting insights at portfolio level.

For pest control, remote mosquito traps and detection systems send live notifications, allowing hotspot identification and more efficient resource deployment. For refuse waste, fill-level sensors, smart bins and RFID tracking trigger full-bin alerts and support route optimisation. For landscape, sensors measuring tree tilt, rainfall and grass height, or drones used in arborist inspections, generate alerts for pruning or removal and support manpower and fleet planning.

For M&E, lift sensors, temperature and occupancy sensors, water leak and water quality sensors, and heat and smoke detectors feed anomaly detection, downtime alerts and user-triggered feedback into maintenance planning. For security, CCTV, video analytics, access control and alarms support incident response, patrol routing and manpower allocation.

At Category 2, the FM company's centralised system acts as a data hub across the supply chain, with feedback management, performance tracking, dashboards and analytics. At Category 3, the building owner aggregates information across the portfolio for benchmarking, asset management, GIS overlays and strategic planning.

## Data management and interoperability

The Guide treats data management as the precondition for Type 3 Smart FM. It identifies three key considerations.

### 1. Ensure data interconnectivity and avoid data silos

Every solution should be assessed for data connectivity before implementation. Systems that run on stand-alone, closed or proprietary protocols create vendor lock-in and hide the data needed for decisions. Rectifying this later can be costly, time-consuming, or require replacing the entire solution.

### 2. Ensure interoperability

Interconnectivity only moves data between systems; interoperability means a system can understand the meaning of data from another system. The Guide uses a BMS point label — "ZP.L1.ACMV.Z1.AIR_FLOW_2.FRESH" — to show how much site-specific convention and domain knowledge is needed to interpret raw building data. The problem grows with multiple smart systems and multiple buildings using different BMS vendors and conventions.

### 3. Implement cybersecurity

Type 3 solutions depend heavily on IT infrastructure, so cybersecurity is a core design consideration. The Guide recommends firewalls that are correctly installed, updated and maintained, strong and regularly changed passwords, antivirus software and prompt patching. It also asks owners to balance security requirements against cost by reviewing the criticality of each system and the sensitivity of its data.

## Common Data Environment, protocols and schemas

### Common Data Environment for FM

A Common Data Environment (CDE) for FM can be established by an FM company or a building owner to manage information during the operational stage of assets. Following ISO 19650-3, the Guide defines a CDE as a combination of workflow and information storage solutions supporting the information management process for the asset.

A CDE is not necessarily one large platform that interoperates with everything. It can be a consolidation of key information from relevant technology stacks, managed through agreed exchange mechanisms. The Guide's example is a CDE that consolidates fault data from an IoT platform and an FM workflow automation platform to support fault detection and diagnostics (FDD), monitoring incident closure time and the mean time between failures of critical assets.

Common CDE features include file and data management, user management, dashboards and reporting, operation and workflow management, open protocols and a cloud-based environment.

### Open communication protocols

Protocols define how devices talk to each other. Proprietary protocols prevent communication without substantial conversion effort and cost, so the Guide recommends mandating open protocols as a foundation.

When choosing a product and protocol, building owners and FM companies should consider:

1. Compatibility with existing systems and equipment.
2. Adoption of the protocol across vendors, which affects flexibility to change systems without disrupting integrations.
3. The need to add devices or systems in future, which favours protocols adopted beyond a single discipline.

The Guide lists five common options:

- **BACnet** — a building automation and control protocol built on the ASHRAE, ANSI and ISO 16484-5 standard, supporting ACMV, lighting, fire protection and access control.
- **Modbus** — widely used across industrial, building, infrastructure, transportation and energy applications.
- **OPC UA** — able to communicate with major building automation protocols such as BACnet and Modbus; the Guide describes it as a universal translator for linking disparate systems.
- **APIs** — software intermediaries that let applications talk to each other, for example when diagnostic software calls a CMMS to create a job ticket.
- **FTP** — an asynchronous file transfer protocol, with SFTP preferred for security; useful for large, non-real-time data transfers.

Data quality also matters. The Guide suggests setting minimum and maximum expected values for measured points so the system can flag anomalies — for example, flagging a room temperature sensor if it reads outside 15°C to 35°C.

### Metadata schemas

Conventions and schemas ensure consistency across systems. This matters when a CDE or central monitoring system must interoperate with multiple BMSes across different properties. Common definitions of how data is named and structured reduce the time, effort and cost of making sense of each building's data.

The Guide points to ISO 21823-3 on semantic interoperability for IoT systems and recommends adopting a common metadata schema. A schema standardises the definition of a "thing" — for example, whether a "zone" means a group of buildings, a group of spaces or a separately controlled cooled space — and the relationships between things, such as an AHU feeding fresh air to a VAV that has a damper.

Organisations should adopt a single schema and stick to it; using different schemas across a portfolio erodes the benefit. Open-source options include:

- **Brick Schema** — semantic descriptions of physical, logical and virtual building assets and their relationships.
- **Project Haystack** — semantic data models and web services for building data.
- **Digital Buildings Project** — a uniform schema and toolset for structured building information, used by Google to manage its portfolio.

### Cybersecurity guidance

The Guide references the IMDA IoT Cyber Security Guide, published in March 2020, including its Annex C case study for smart buildings. It also points to Technical Reference 111 on securing cyber-physical systems for buildings, and to the Cyber Security Agency's Cyber Essentials and Cyber Trust marks, which recognise organisations with essential cyber hygiene and comprehensive cybersecurity practices respectively.

## Data use cases

The Guide describes several use cases that become possible once data is managed across systems.

**Workflow tracking and service level delivery** consolidates data on works detected and carried out across one or more buildings. Rather than every owner buying and maintaining a CMMS, owners can use the FM company's CMMS and a cloud data platform to receive completion duration, cost and material or part replacement data for reporting, tracking and analytics.

**Fault detection and diagnostics (FDD)** detects deviations from normal operation and diagnoses the type and location of the problem. The Guide notes that equipment data is often locked inside a BMS, especially across multiple BMS brands, and that centralising raw data can be costly. One response is to process and consolidate data at the edge before sending it to a CDE — for example, sending daily averages rather than per-second readings when the use case is annual analysis.

**Condition-based maintenance** observes and measures an asset over time to identify potential failure before it happens. Techniques include vibration analysis, infrared and thermal analysis, and electrical analysis with clamp-on ammeters. The Guide advises focusing on the most critical assets for the best return on investment.

## Workforce and digital skills

The Guide argues that technology adoption fails when people are not equipped to use it. It warns that a lack of understanding of when, what and how to use a tool can produce frustration and confusion, leading staff to avoid digitalisation altogether. The principle it states is: "Use the right tool for the right job."

It recommends a four-step process for building digital capabilities:

1. Identify the required skillsets based on business needs.
2. Map the required skillsets to the role the user performs.
3. Find suitable methods for users to develop the skillsets.
4. Practise, refine and sharpen.

The FMIC and its taskforces mapped the relevant digital skillsets to 10 Technical Skills and Competencies (TSCs) under the Skills Framework:

1. Technology Scanning
2. Robotic and Automation Technology Application
3. Internet of Things Management
4. Technology Application
5. Applications Integration
6. Smart Facilities Management
7. Data Collection and Analysis
8. Design Thinking Practice
9. Integrated Digital Delivery Application
10. Cybersecurity

The Guide warns against a "one size fit all" approach that overloads operational staff with capabilities outside their scope. Instead, proficiency should be tailored to the role: "The right person, in the right job, with the right skills, at the right time." It maps job roles into senior management, middle management and operational teams, and describes progression from basic to intermediate to advanced levels, with subject matter experts coaching less experienced staff.

## Case study: CBM digiHUB

CBM Pte Ltd is an integrated facilities management provider with more than 50 years of experience and a subsidiary of City Developments Limited (CDL). It developed digiHUB, a Smart Properties Management System that integrates predictive maintenance sensors, BMS, CMMS, visitor management, video analytics, security robotics and toilet monitoring into a common platform.

The deployment covered a portfolio of commercial and retail developments in Singapore under an outcome-based IFM and AFM framework. More than 2,500 IoT sensors were deployed progressively across multiple developments, covering ACMV equipment, pumps, cooling towers, electrical systems, visitor management, cleaning and security.

The starting point was manual: paper checklists, fixed preventive maintenance schedules, manual security monitoring and physical patrols, with no central visibility across FM functions. digiHUB was implemented through site assessments, infrastructure reviews, system integration, user acceptance testing, staff training and operational handover.

Key elements of the implementation:

- **Predictive maintenance.** Sensors monitor vibration, temperature, sound levels and equipment status. Analytics and machine learning identify abnormal patterns and degradation before failure. For selected equipment, servicing moved from monthly to bi-monthly cycles while maintaining reliability.
- **Ops-tech integration.** Existing standard operating procedures were mapped against alert types. Escalation thresholds were set by equipment criticality, response time targets were defined, and alerts were routed to field teams or the command centre. All actions were tracked from initiation to closure.
- **Change management.** Over 100 operational staff were trained across engineering, cleaning, security and command centre functions. Training was role-specific and included hands-on practice with live data and simulated alerts.
- **Phased rollout.** Technologies were introduced sequentially to manage complexity and maintain operational stability.

The reported productivity improvements were:

| Function | Solution | Productivity improvement |
|----------|----------|--------------------------|
| Engineering | IoT sensors for predictive maintenance | 50% |
| FM process | Auto-ticketing system | 90.8% |
| Cleaning | Toilet monitoring system | 20.5% |
| Visitor management | Self-service kiosk | 100% |
| Security | Virtual patrol, video analytics and robotics | 20% |

Collectively, these contributed to an overall productivity improvement of approximately 23% to 25% across the developments. CBM's business case projected cumulative cost savings of approximately S$11.3 million over an 11-year period through manpower optimisation, workflow automation and improved maintenance effectiveness.

The case's lessons are consistent with the Guide's wider argument: technology adoption is ultimately a human challenge, ops-tech integration must be grounded in existing workflows, and phased rollout with centralised dashboards supports both adoption and governance.

## Case study: Ecovolt plug-load management at CDL

Ecovolt Technologies builds an AI-native Building Operating System called Everest, with smart-plug hardware called Volta Plug. The case study applies the system to plug-load standby waste in a Grade A office owned by CDL.

The problem is one that conventional metering misses. Building- or floor-level meters cannot show how much individual devices consume, or distinguish active use from standby waste. Equipment left switched on continues to draw power whether or not anyone is present. In an office occupied for roughly a third of the week, a large share of plug-load energy can be consumed while the space is empty. The waste is small per device but large across many devices and buildings.

The deployment had four outcomes: energy savings, better visibility and asset insight, productivity and convenience, and sustainability and compliance. It used device-level metering and remote switching through Volta Plug, an analytics engine that learns each device's usage pattern, and open protocols to integrate with CDL's wider systems.

The installation was non-intrusive: Volta Plug sits between a standard socket outlet and the equipment, requiring no electrical works, no rewiring and no Licensed Electrical Worker. The commercial model was a hybrid CAPEX-OPEX structure — one-time capital expenditure for hardware, followed by a fixed annual subscription for the software platform, cloud hosting, monitoring, analytics and support.

The measurement used matched 14-day windows, each with ten working days and four weekend days. Over those windows, consumption on the monitored plug-load circuits fell from **192.88 kWh to 137.15 kWh**, a reduction of **55.74 kWh or 28.9%**. Average daily consumption fell from **13.78 kWh to 9.80 kWh**.

| Metric | Before Ecovolt | After Ecovolt | Reduction |
|--------|----------------|---------------|-----------|
| Energy consumed (kWh) | 192.88 | 137.15 | 55.74 (28.9%) |
| Average daily energy (kWh) | 13.78 | 9.80 | 3.98 (28.9%) |
| Carbon emissions (kg CO₂) | 78.80 | 56.02 | 22.77 (28.9%) |
| Projected annual energy (kWh) | 5,029.12 | 3,575.61 | 1,453.50 |
| Projected annual carbon (kg CO₂) | 2,054.39 | 1,460.64 | 593.76 |

Carbon emissions were derived using a grid emission factor of approximately 0.40 kg CO₂ per kWh. The projected annual figures extrapolate from the matched windows and assume the windows are representative of the full year.

The savings were concentrated when the office was unoccupied. Overnight, plug-load consumption fell by about 85%; during working hours it fell by about 19%; at weekends it fell by about 80%. In the hourly table, unoccupied hours (19:00 to 08:00) fell from 2.12 kWh per day to 0.33 kWh per day, a reduction of 84.5%, while working hours (08:00 to 19:00) fell from 11.65 kWh per day to 9.47 kWh per day, a reduction of 18.8%.

Ecovolt reports that customers typically see a payback period under 2 years, including CAPEX and OPEX, because the solution requires no additional cabling, wiring or trunking.

## What the Guide adds up to

- **Smart FM is an operating model, not a product.** The definition combines systems, processes, technologies and personnel, and the Guide repeatedly returns to process and people as the constraints on technology value.
- **Integration and aggregation are distinct but linked.** Integration bundles services; aggregation pools demand and resources. Both are needed to make outcome-based IFM and AFM contracts work.
- **The three types are a classification, not a ladder.** Type 1, 2 and 3 describe the scope of "smartness", not maturity. A Type 1 solution can be the right answer if the cost-benefit case supports it.
- **Contract design determines technology adoption.** Longer outcome-based contracts — at least three years plus a three-year extension, and five to ten years in some public agencies — give providers the certainty to invest.
- **Data management is the bottleneck for Type 3.** Open protocols, a CDE, common metadata schemas and cybersecurity are the preconditions for integration across systems.
- **The workforce is the delivery mechanism.** The Guide maps digital skills to roles and warns against a one-size-fits-all training approach.
- **The case studies show the range.** CBM's digiHUB demonstrates a portfolio-scale integrated ecosystem with measurable productivity and cost outcomes; Ecovolt demonstrates a narrow, non-intrusive intervention with measured energy and carbon savings.
