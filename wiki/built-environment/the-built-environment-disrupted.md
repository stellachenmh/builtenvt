## Overview

The built environment sector is being reshaped by four overlapping disruptions: climate change, an ageing workforce, resource scarcity and the COVID-19 pandemic. Rather than treating these as separate problems, the cases in this article show how technology, design and nature-based solutions can turn disruption into an opportunity to build cities that are more sustainable and resilient. The examples range from solar canals and energy-positive buildings to circular construction, mangrove restoration, robotic delivery, automated dam-building and mass engineered timber.

## Disruptions facing the sector

- **Climate change** — pressure to cut emissions and adapt to hazards such as urban flooding.
- **An ageing workforce** — construction faces labour shortages and a need to make the industry more attractive to younger workers.
- **Resource scarcity** — limited land, materials and energy require more efficient use and closed loops.
- **COVID-19** — demand for contactless services and reduced disease transmission risk.

## Case studies

### Maximising synergies in infrastructure — Gujarat, India

Solar panels suspended over a canal show how one piece of infrastructure can serve two systems at once. The panels shade the water, reducing evaporation and leaving more water for nearby residents and crops. In return, the water below cools the panels, improving their efficiency. The arrangement addresses limited land resources while linking water supply and energy generation.

### Net-positive sustainable energy buildings — Trondheim, Norway

Powerhouse Brattørkaia is described as the world's northernmost energy-positive building. Its sloped roof carries solar panels that allow it to generate twice as much energy as it consumes daily, even in challenging conditions. The surplus is fed into the electrical grid for neighbouring buildings, electric buses, cars and boats. The case points beyond self-sufficiency: as solar technology improves, buildings can become net contributors to the surrounding energy system.

### Applying circular economy principles — Eindhoven, The Netherlands

The People's Pavilion, built for Dutch Design Week 2017, demonstrated circularity in both construction and disassembly. It used borrowed or recycled materials, including recycled plastic for the colourful shingles on its cladding. After use, the building was dismantled and its materials were returned to their respective suppliers, closing the loop in the value chain. The lesson is to incorporate circularity upstream, during planning and design, so that buildings can reduce waste and keep resources in use.

### Restoring nature through design — Sanya, China

Sanya Mangrove Park rehabilitated mangroves that had been destroyed by rapid urban development. Designers used an "interlocking fingers" layout that channeled sea tides into the park while mitigating strong storm currents that could damage new mangrove plantings. The project shows that nature-based solutions and design strategies can be as effective as high technology for challenges such as urban flood risk, while also creating public spaces for communities.

### Robotic delivery services — Fairfax, United States of America

Starship Technologies delivery robots at George Mason University in Fairfax, Virginia, provide contactless delivery. That service became more sought after during the COVID-19 pandemic because it reduces disease transmission risk between people. In the longer run, robotic delivery may be a more sustainable and affordable alternative to traditional delivery vehicles, easing traffic congestion and vehicular carbon emissions while improving on-demand delivery.

### Automation to address labour constraints — Mie Prefecture, Japan

The Japan Water Agency used robotics and automation heavily in a trial project to construct a dam in Mie Prefecture. Automation reduces the strain on an ageing workforce by lowering manpower needs, speeding up repetitive work, taking on dangerous tasks and even allowing work through the night. The approach can raise overall productivity and make construction more attractive to younger workers.

### Innovative construction materials — Singapore

The Wave, a sports hall at Nanyang Technological University, is the first large-scale building in Southeast Asia constructed using Mass Engineered Timber (MET). Its MET comes only from Forest Stewardship Council-certified sources and acts as a carbon sink by sequestering carbon in the wood. MET's high strength-to-weight ratio makes it easier to handle, enabling faster and greener construction, and it has a much smaller environmental footprint than reinforced concrete or steel. The hall can host three full-sized basketball courts or 13 badminton courts.

## Cross-cutting lessons

- **Combine technology with design.** High-tech solutions such as solar panels and robotics sit alongside low-tech, nature-based approaches such as mangrove restoration.
- **Design for the whole system.** Solar canals, net-positive buildings and circular pavilions all create value beyond a single site or user.
- **Treat resources as loops, not lines.** Borrowing, recycling and returning materials upstream in the design process reduces waste.
- **Use disruption as a catalyst.** Labour shortages, pandemic constraints and climate risks are prompting innovations that can improve productivity, resilience and liveability at the same time.
