# AI Adoption and Governance in the Built Environment

> Sources: Pinsent Masons, "The challenge of AI adoption in the built environment sector", 31 October 2025; Pinsent Masons, "AI adoption in built environment 'requires mindset shift'", 15 October 2025
> Raw: [The challenge of AI adoption in the built environment sector](../../raw/built-environment/2025-10-31-ai-adoption-challenge-built-environment.md); [AI adoption in built environment 'requires mindset shift'](../../raw/built-environment/2025-10-15-ai-adoption-mindset-shift.md)
> Updated: 2026-11-10

## Overview

A global survey and a London roundtable run by Pinsent Masons with Bentley Systems, Mott MacDonald and Turner & Townsend found that the built environment sector sees AI as transformative but is not yet organised to adopt it at scale. The survey ran between February and June 2025 and drew **86 respondents**, **75%** of them at c-suite or board level, primarily from the UK, North America and Australia, with some responses from Europe, Asia, the Middle East and Africa. The roundtable in early October brought together **40** leaders and senior executives from government, investment, legal, technology, project and cost management, engineering design, contracting, client and developer organisations.

The consensus was that AI would initially have an incremental impact, with significant disruption expected once its benefits are more widely felt. The analysis is blunt about what has to change: the sector needs significant investment to move from a siloed, fragmented model to a more integrated, digital and data-driven one.

> Significant levels of investment will be needed to change a siloed and heavily fragmented industry to a more integrated, modern, digital and data-driven business model.

— Ian Laing, Partner, Head of Infrastructure & Real Estate, Pinsent Masons

## Readiness and maturity

The survey paints a picture of broad awareness but limited operational maturity.

- **40%** of businesses anticipate that AI-driven change will significantly affect their current business model.
- A further **24%** treat AI-driven change as a major consideration and are already taking active steps to adapt their business model.
- Only **20%** said AI is of current strategic importance to them or that they have plans to adopt it more widely across the organisation.
- **36%** reported using generic AI systems such as Copilot.
- **26%** said they were already using and trialling AI systems with specific use cases in mind.
- **15%** said they had selectively used AI or trialled applications but had not yet integrated AI into operations.

The sector is therefore experimenting, but much of that experimentation is generic rather than bespoke. The analysis describes the sector as "nascent" in its readiness to develop, adopt and use bespoke AI solutions.

### Designers are ahead of contractors

The survey found a marked split between professional groups.

- **Two-thirds** of architectural and engineering designers were either already taking active steps to adapt or saw significant future disruption to their operational business models.
- Only **12%** of contractors were actively taking steps to adapt their current business models for future disruption.
- **35%** of contractors expected AI to have a significant impact on their business model in the next three years, but **over 50%** anticipated a more limited impact or no impact at all.

Graham Robinson of Pinsent Masons warned that contractors should not assume on-site automation is distant: "While practitioners are sceptical about on-site automation in the short-term, the long-term potential remains high and contractors that ignore this potential may become casualties of the rapid advancement of AI." He suggested incremental pilots such as autonomous drones for site surveys or AI for safety monitoring.

## Where AI is being used now

The most common uses reported by respondents were:

- automating document-related processes;
- real-time monitoring of operational data to predict maintenance needs or optimise asset performance and resilience;
- optimising construction productivity.

Where organisations are investing more deliberately, the focus is on design and engineering productivity, including generative design, complex simulation, digital twinning and rapid analysis of design options. Cost estimation, forecasting and real-time tracking of site progress are also identified as strong areas of current or expected investment.

The analysis argues that digitalisation of engineering design and standardisation of data across the supply chain must happen before automation of site activities can scale.

Agentic AI — systems that use AI agents and other tools to reason, plan and self-evaluate — is not yet widely used. The analysis cites a knowledge gap and unease about agentic systems, compounded by concerns about jobs.

## Barriers to adoption

The analysis identifies five broad barriers.

### 1. Limited investment capacity

Construction profit margins leave little headroom for investment compared with other sectors. Firms want evidence that a solution works before committing, because a failed investment can be very costly.

### 2. Fragmentation and localised delivery

Adoption depends on location, business size and readiness. Differing standards and regulations across regions and sectors make it harder to standardise AI models.

### 3. Supply-chain discontinuity

Construction is not a continuous production line. Projects stop and start, supply chains are fragmented, and designs, specialisms and products vary from project to project. That makes it difficult to build reusable data and AI capability.

### 4. Data security, bias and trust

A risk-averse industry is wary of data security, model bias and trust. Operational silos, a lack of standardisation and non-machine-readable data all hinder adoption. One executive quoted in the analysis said much industry information is still only in people's heads, let alone in a database.

### 5. AI literacy and leadership

AI literacy and leadership are described as critical. Successful projects require investment in people, workforce and skills to create an AI-ready organisation.

## Risks and governance

The survey found a governance gap that sits awkwardly beside the sector's safety responsibilities.

- **60%** of organisations said they had some kind of AI policy in place.
- **20%** had implemented a general organisation-wide AI policy.
- **6%** had specific policies for individual departments covering use, governance, ethical implications, safety measures and related aspects, with plans to update them.
- **12%** had no department-specific or organisation-wide AI policies at all.
- **37%** said they had limited or no project controls to manage risk where AI is used for design and construction.

The analysis describes AI adoption as being treated with "gold rush fever" in some quarters. Without a clear vision, an adoption roadmap and a robust governance framework, the risk of expensive mistakes rises.

Other risks include:

- **Health and safety.** AI can produce plausible but incorrect results, which in construction could lead to flawed risk assessments or incorrect safety recommendations.
- **Supply-chain cyber risk.** Greater use of AI and technology increases exposure to cybercrime and can affect business resilience.
- **Data quality and security.** Poor inputs produce poor outputs. Data must be machine-readable, standardised and trustworthy, and commercially sensitive information and intellectual property need protection.
- **Bias and opacity.** AI may reinforce bias or operate outside legal and ethical boundaries. "Black box" systems that show inputs and outputs but not the weighting of factors can undermine trust.
- **Erosion of human oversight.** Over-reliance on AI may weaken critical human skills.

Robinson's prescription is a combination of model validation, peer review of AI-assisted designs, and clear policies and contractual terms:

> Without adequate governance and project controls, AI mistakes could pose safety risks or legal liabilities.

He also suggested regulators may need to update guidelines to cover AI-assisted engineering.

## Opportunities

The analysis is not only a risk register. It argues the construction industry is ripe for disruption because projects run late, costs exceed expectations, productivity has suffered from complexity, and skills are scarce.

Specific opportunities include:

- **Design and engineering optimisation** — generative design, complex simulation, digital twins and rapid option analysis.
- **Responsive project environments** — integrating AI with IoT sensors and BIM so decisions are based on real-time data rather than a static plan.
- **Contract management** — fewer people and better-quality outputs.
- **Cost estimation and forecasting** — predictive analysis using data, statistics, modelling and machine learning.
- **Fault prediction** — identifying faults and performance degradation in buildings and infrastructure.
- **Safety monitoring** — drones, real-time tracking and AI-assisted robotics.
- **Data interpretation** — AI analysis of lidar scans, photography and site surveys.
- **Data monetisation** — selling data or using it internally to improve operations, products and decisions.
- **Regulatory compliance and sustainability** — monitoring energy, reducing waste, improving material forecasting, ESG reporting, and supporting the "golden thread" of building safety information across the lifecycle.

The analysis also argues that AI could drive greater virtual integration of construction supply chains by consolidating processes, improving data-driven decisions and giving organisations more control across the value chain.

## What leaders should do

Pinsent Masons sets out five areas for business leaders:

1. **Develop a clear vision and roadmap** for how AI might disrupt how built assets are developed and operated, and keep it under review as the technology changes.
2. **Put governance and risk management in place**, particularly around data standards, ethics, data-sharing protocols, cybersecurity, business resilience and intellectual property.
3. **Build standardised, machine-readable and actionable data**, with common data environments shared across projects in the way advanced manufacturing industries share data.
4. **Invest in people and talent**, including recruiting digital engineers and upskilling the wider workforce.
5. **Create safe environments for exploration and learning**, because trialling new technology on live, safety-critical projects is difficult.

The analysis adds two cross-cutting requirements: continuous human oversight to check accuracy and reduce bias, and a mindset shift backed by significant investment in digital skills and training.

> Expecting a jump to a majority or higher levels projects where AI is significantly leveraged in three years will require a significant shift in the construction industry's conservatism and culture and the actual pace of adoption may be slower if challenges are not addressed.

— Graham Robinson

## What the evidence does and does not show

The survey is a senior-level, self-reported snapshot rather than a measure of realised productivity outcomes. Its **86** respondents are concentrated in the UK, North America and Australia, so the findings should not be treated as a representative global census. What it does show clearly is a gap between expectation and readiness: many leaders expect significant disruption, but far fewer have moved from generic experimentation to governed, data-ready, use-case-specific adoption.

That gap is the practical agenda. AI use cases are relatively easy to describe; the constraints are investment capacity, fragmented data, supply-chain discontinuity, governance and skills.
