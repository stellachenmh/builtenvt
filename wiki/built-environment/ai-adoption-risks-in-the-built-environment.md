# AI Adoption Risks in the Built Environment

> Sources: RICS, "Artificial intelligence in construction report 2025"; MIT Project NANDA, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025"; Gartner press releases and analysis, 2024–2026; Dodge Construction Network and CMiC, "Contractor Optimism About AI's Transformative Impact", December 2025; Bluebeam AEC research, October 2025; RIBA AI Reports 2025 and 2026; Autodesk and FMI bad-data research, 2021; Cyberhaven AI risk research, 2025–2026; IBM, "Cost of a Data Breach" 2025 and 2026; McKinsey, "The state of AI in 2025"; World Economic Forum, "Future of Jobs Report 2025"; Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University, CHI 2025; IEA, "Energy and AI", 2025; for mitigation evidence: Writer AI strategy survey 2025; Thomson Reuters, "Future of Professionals Report 2025"; Pacific AI / Gradient Flow, "2025 AI Governance Survey"; Cloud Security Alliance shadow-AI research, 2026; Microsoft and LinkedIn, "Work Trend Index 2024"; AWS and Access Partnership AI-skills research; LinkedIn Learning, "Workplace Learning Report 2025"; NCCER, "From Training to Performance", 2025; Dell'Acqua et al., Organization Science, 2026

## Overview

This article tests four shortlisted risks of adopting AI in the built environment against quantitative evidence — poor-quality outputs from poor-quality data inputs, lack of AI governance policies, lack of staff knowledge on how to use and supervise AI tools, and low or no ROI from adopting inappropriate tools — and then asks what other risks the evidence suggests adding to the register. A final section sets out two to three mitigation steps for each of the four risks, each supported by quantitative evidence of effectiveness.

Two kinds of quantitative evidence are available. The first is **stakeholder surveys**: construction and AEC professionals asked directly which risks and barriers are biggest. The second is **outcome data**: studies measuring what share of AI programmes fail, and why. The two tell a consistent story. Stakeholders rank skills, integration and data quality as the biggest barriers; outcome studies show that most AI programmes still deliver no measurable financial return, and that the root causes of failure are precisely data readiness, weak governance, a workforce learning gap and unclear business value. In other words, the four shortlisted risks are the same failure modes the failure statistics point to — they differ mainly in how directly each can be quantified.

## What stakeholders rank as the biggest risks

The largest survey of construction professionals is RICS's 2025 report, based on **2,200+** professionals worldwide. Asked for their top three challenges to AI adoption:

- **46%** — lack of skilled personnel (most cited)
- **37%** — integration with existing systems
- **30%** — data quality and availability
- **29%** — high implementation costs
- **28%** — unclear return on investment
- **22%** — privacy and security concerns
- **11%** — regulatory or legal uncertainty

Other stakeholder surveys fill in the picture:

- **Dodge Construction Network and CMiC (2025, US contractors):** **57%** are concerned about the accuracy of AI output, **54%** about data security, and only **26%** rate the quality of their existing data as high. **87%** believe AI will have a meaningful impact on construction, but only **19%** have adapted their workflows for it.
- **Bluebeam (2025, 1,000+ AEC professionals):** the most common AI integration challenge is data-sharing security (**42%**), ahead of cost and complexity (**33%**); **69%** say concerns about potential AI regulation have affected their AI efforts; **23%** find it hard to keep up with rapidly changing technologies.
- **Autodesk (2025):** **55%** of construction leaders cite lack of skilled talent as a barrier to growth, up from **43%** a year earlier.
- **Eide Bailly / Construction Dive:** **45%** of leaders lack clarity on where to begin, **21%** have security and governance concerns, **18%** are uncertain of AI's value, **17%** face data-quality challenges.
- **Pinsent Masons, Bentley Systems, Mott MacDonald and Turner & Townsend (2025, 86 senior respondents):** only **20%** had an organisation-wide AI policy, and **37%** had limited or no project controls where AI is used for design and construction. See [AI Adoption and Governance in the Built Environment](ai-adoption-in-the-built-environment.md).

Adoption context matters for interpreting these numbers. RICS found **45%** of organisations had no AI implementation at all and fewer than **12%** used AI regularly in specific processes; RIBA found **74%** of UK architecture practices now use AI on at least some projects, but only **15%** day-to-day. Most of the sector is therefore still exposed to adoption risk before it is exposed to operations risk.

## What outcome data shows

- **MIT Project NANDA (2025):** despite **$30–40 billion** of enterprise generative AI investment, **95%** of organisations saw zero measurable P&L return; only **5%** of integrated pilots extracted real value. The report attributes the divide to approach rather than model quality: a workforce learning gap, failure to integrate with workflows, and the absence of a defined outcome before building. Buying tools from specialised vendors or partners succeeded about **67%** of the time; internal builds succeeded only about a third as often.
- **Gartner (2024–2026):** at least **30%** of GenAI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by end of 2025, due to poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs or unclear business value — and Gartner's January 2026 analysis of hundreds of implementations found the actual figure was **at least 50%**, with lack of business value the number-one failure point. Separately, Gartner predicts organisations will abandon **60%** of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data through 2026, while **63%** of organisations lack, or are unsure they have, the right data management practices for AI.
- **S&P Global Market Intelligence (2025, 1,000+ firms):** the average organisation scrapped **46%** of AI proofs-of-concept before production; **42%** of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives.
- **McKinsey (2025–2026):** **88%** of organisations use AI in at least one function, but only **39%** report any enterprise-level EBIT impact from it, and only about **6%** qualify as high performers.
- **RIBA AI Report 2026:** among architecture practices using AI, **73%** report improved productivity and **57%** of those assessing ROI report a positive return — but only **17%** agree their designs are better for it, and only **7%** say AI has enabled them to increase fees.

## The four shortlisted risks against the evidence

### 1. Poor-quality outputs from poor-quality data inputs

**Verdict: strongly supported, and it is the most structural of the four in construction.**

Poor data quality is the first-listed cause in Gartner's abandonment analysis, and Gartner's AI-ready-data prediction (**60%** of unsupported projects abandoned through 2026) makes the mechanism explicit. Construction's data foundations are unusually weak for an AI-dependent technology:

- Autodesk and FMI estimated bad data may have cost the global construction industry **$1.8–1.85 trillion** in 2020, including **$88.69 billion** of avoidable rework (**14%** of all rework); **30%** of professionals say more than half their project data is bad.
- **95–96%** of data captured in engineering and construction goes unused (FMI), and construction professionals spend **35%** of working hours looking for data, resolving conflicts and dealing with rework (PlanGrid/FMI).
- **52%** of rework worldwide is attributed to poor project data and miscommunication (PlanGrid/FMI); only **26%** of US contractors rate their existing data quality as high (Dodge/CMiC); **76%** of general contractors report data integration challenges (Dodge).
- Poor data quality costs the average organisation **$12.9 million** a year across industries (Gartner).

See [Data Readiness for AI in Construction](data-readiness-for-ai-in-construction.md) for why construction data is hard for AI to use even before quality is considered.

### 2. Lack of AI governance policies

**Verdict: strongly supported; the governance gap is well documented and in some measures widening.**

- Only **19%** of UK architecture practices have a documented AI policy (RIBA 2026) — though **56%** of practices with 50+ staff do, meaning smaller practices carry the exposure. In the wider built environment survey by Pinsent Masons et al., only **20%** had an organisation-wide AI policy and **37%** lacked project controls for AI used in design and construction.
- Across industries: **75%** of organisations have AI usage policies but only **36%** a formal governance framework (Pacific AI 2025); only **43%** of businesses have an AI governance policy at all (PEX 2025/26); only **8%** of AI-using organisations maintain a comprehensive governance framework (Economist Impact); **80%** still need AI risk-management controls (EY).
- The gap is not closing: IBM found only **32%** of breached organisations had AI policies in 2026, down from **37%**, while breaches involving unsanctioned "shadow AI" more than doubled from **20%** to **43%** of breached organisations.
- Governance is a named cause of AI project failure in Gartner's analysis ("inadequate risk controls"), and it mediates most other risks on this page: leakage, liability, safety review, IP handling.

### 3. Lack of staff knowledge on using and supervising AI tools

**Verdict: the most-cited barrier in the largest construction-sector survey, and the risk with the clearest evidence of harm.**

- **46%** of RICS respondents named lack of skilled personnel as a top barrier — the single most-cited answer. **55%** of construction leaders cite lack of skilled talent as a barrier to growth (Autodesk, up from 43%); **45%** of leaders lack clarity on where to begin (Eide Bailly); **23%** find it hard to keep up with rapidly changing technologies (Bluebeam).
- The readiness gap shows in behaviour: **87%** of US contractors believe AI will matter, but only **19%** have adapted workflows (Dodge/CMiC).
- Supervision capability specifically: Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon (CHI 2025, 319 knowledge workers) found that higher confidence in generative AI correlates with **less** critical thinking (β = −0.69, p < 0.001), while higher self-confidence correlates with more — meaning staff who trust the tool most scrutinise it least. MIT Media Lab's EEG study found LLM-assisted writers showed the weakest neural engagement and poor recall of their own work, persisting after they switched to unaided writing ("cognitive debt").
- Unsupervised use is measurable: **77%** of employees who use AI have pasted company data into ChatGPT-type tools, **82%** of them from personal accounts invisible to IT (LayerX). MIT NANDA describes the same phenomenon as a "shadow AI economy".

### 4. Low or no ROI from adopting inappropriate tools

**Verdict: the best-quantified risk in absolute terms, though the failure statistics are cross-industry rather than construction-specific.**

- **95%** of organisations saw zero measurable P&L return from GenAI despite $30–40 billion invested (MIT NANDA); at least **50%** of GenAI projects are abandoned after proof of concept (Gartner, January 2026); the average organisation scraps **46%** of AI PoCs (S&P Global); only **39%** report any EBIT impact, and mostly under 5% (McKinsey).
- The failure causes are the user's other three risks: Gartner lists poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value; MIT NANDA lists the learning gap and missing workflow integration.
- Hype is a documented driver: Gartner's agentic AI research describes most agentic projects as "early stage experiments or proof of concepts that are mostly driven by hype and are often misapplied", with over **40%** forecast to be cancelled by end of 2027.
- In built-environment terms: **28%** of RICS respondents cite unclear ROI and **29%** implementation cost; **18%** of construction leaders are uncertain of AI's value (Eide Bailly). RIBA's productivity-without-better-designs pattern (**73%** faster, **17%** better) is the sector-specific version of efficiency gains that do not reach outcomes or fees.
- Counterweight for balance: where adoption is working, returns are real — **68%** of AI-using AEC firms report saving at least $50,000 and **46%** report saving 500–1,000 hours (Bluebeam). The risk is concentrated in tool-led adoption rather than problem-led adoption.

## Additional risks the evidence suggests adding

**Confidentiality leakage and shadow AI.** The share of corporate data employees feed to AI tools that is sensitive tripled in two years, from **10.7%** to **34.8%** (Cyberhaven); one in five organisations has suffered a breach linked to shadow AI, adding about **$670,000** to the average breach cost (IBM 2025); shadow-AI incidents doubled again to **43%** of breached organisations in 2026. Project data, drawings and cost models are exactly the kind of confidential material at issue. This risk compounds the governance and skills risks above.

**Cybersecurity exposure.** Construction has been ranked the **#1 most-targeted industry** for cyberattacks (NordLocker); ransomware attacks on construction rose **48%** from 2022 to 2023 (Corvus); **59%** of firms experienced a cybersecurity threat within two years (Dodge/Egnyte); only **20%** of construction decision-makers claim cyber knowledge and only **30%** have business continuity plans (Travelers). AI widens the attack surface and the phishing content it enables.

**Intellectual property and imitation.** **76%** of architects believe AI increases the risk of their work being imitated (RIBA 2026, up from 69% in 2025); IP was exposed in **40%** of shadow-AI breaches and carried the highest per-record cost (IBM). Training AI tools on scraped drawings, or feeding client-confidential designs into third-party models, are live exposure routes.

**Hallucination and accuracy in safety-critical work.** **57%** of US contractors are concerned about AI accuracy (Dodge/CMiC). The concern is quantified elsewhere: general-purpose LLMs hallucinate **58–88%** of the time on verifiable professional queries (Stanford, legal domain); even purpose-built RAG tools hallucinate **17–33%**; GPT-4 answered only **76.6%** of civil Fundamentals-of-Engineering exam questions correctly. In a safety-critical sector, "plausible but incorrect" outputs can become flawed risk assessments and incorrect safety recommendations (Pinsent Masons).

**Professional liability and insurance.** RIBA 2026 records architects' concerns that professional indemnity insurance may not respond to AI-related errors, and that AI use may affect premiums. Accountability for professional work remains with the professional regardless of the tool used (Beale & Co).

**Regulatory exposure.** EU AI Act enforcement for general-purpose AI models, prohibited practices and transparency rules began in August 2026, with Annex III high-risk obligations from December 2027 and fines up to **€35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover**. **69%** of AEC firms say regulation concerns have already affected their AI plans (Bluebeam).

**Overreliance and deskilling of the talent pipeline.** **61%** of architects believe AI will make it harder for early-career professionals to gain skills, because the routine tasks being automated are how juniors learn (RIBA 2026). The Microsoft/CMU and MIT Media Lab findings above are the mechanism; in a sector with a documented shortage of skilled talent, erosion of the training pipeline is a compounding risk.

**Workforce displacement and morale.** By 2030 the WEF projects **92 million** jobs displaced globally against **170 million** created (22% churn); McKinsey estimates AI could automate **39%** of nonphysical work in construction and **50%** in architecture and engineering; **44%** of project managers are concerned about AI's impact on their role (RICS). Retention and trust suffer when this is handled badly.

**Integration with legacy systems and workflow disruption.** **37%** of RICS respondents name integration as a barrier (second only to skills), **76%** of general contractors report data integration challenges, and 20 of 23 AI-enabled project management functions are used by fewer than **15%** of contractors (Dodge/CMiC). Poor integration is both a cost risk and the reason efficiency gains fail to reach the business.

**Energy footprint and sustainability claims.** Data centres consumed about **415 TWh** in 2024 and are projected to reach around **945 TWh** by 2030, with US data centres accounting for nearly half of US electricity demand growth (IEA). For a sector whose transformation agenda is built on decarbonisation, unexamined AI compute sits awkwardly with net-zero commitments and ESG reporting.

## Which risks are biggest?

Triangulating the two evidence types:

| Evidence type | Points to |
| --- | --- |
| Stakeholder surveys (RICS, Dodge, Bluebeam, Autodesk) | Skills first (46%), integration second (37%), data quality third (30%); security and accuracy concerns majority-level in the US surveys |
| Outcome studies (MIT NANDA, Gartner, S&P, McKinsey) | ROI failure is the most common realised harm (95% zero P&L return; ≥50% abandoned after PoC; 39% EBIT impact) |
| Root-cause analyses (Gartner, MIT NANDA) | Data quality, governance/risk controls, learning gap and unclear business value — the four shortlisted risks |

The synthesis is that the four shortlisted risks are not competing hypotheses but a causal chain: **hype-driven tool selection** (risk 4) applied by a **workforce without the skills to supervise it** (risk 3) on top of **data the organisation has not made AI-ready** (risk 1), without **governance to catch the errors** (risk 2), produces the no-ROI outcome the failure statistics measure. On the numbers alone, skills is the most defensible "biggest risk" to cite for construction specifically (46%, the top answer in the largest sector survey), ROI failure is the best-quantified outcome (95%/≥50%/39%), and data quality is the most construction-specific structural weakness (a $1.85 trillion problem before AI is added to it). Two under-appreciated risks deserve promotion from the evidence: **confidentiality leakage via shadow AI** (34.8% of data now sensitive; one-in-five breach rate) and **hallucination in safety-critical use** (17–88% error rates on professional queries) — both are quantified, both are direct functions of the governance and skills gaps, and both carry consequences the built environment is poorly placed to absorb.

## Mitigating the four risks: what the evidence supports

The mitigation evidence comes in two strengths, and each step below says which it rests on. **Direct outcome evidence** means an experiment or a measured difference attributable to the mitigation (the BCG field experiment, construction training outcomes, breach-cost data). **Causal-chain evidence** means the risk statistics above identify a specific cause, and the step removes that cause — strong support, but not the same as measuring the mitigation itself. Construction-specific outcome data for AI mitigations is thin; the strongest construction numbers available are the NCCER craft-training outcomes and the Autodesk/FMI data-strategy correlations, both of which pre-date AI tools but measure the same mechanism: trained people and governed data produce better field outcomes.

### Risk 1 — poor-quality outputs from poor-quality data inputs

1. **Standardise data at source inside a common data environment, and measure data quality.** The firms that always use project data in decision-making — **12%** of the Autodesk/FMI sample — do three things at much higher rates than average: review data for quality (**40%**), standardise collection and reporting (**38%**) and structure data in a common data environment (**38%**); they reported fewer delays, budget overruns, change orders, safety incidents and less rework. **58%** of contractors already use standardised inputs on at least half their projects, and **41%** agree non-standardised input produces unusable data. Yet **59%** of organisations do not measure data quality at all — measurement is the prerequisite. *(Causal-chain evidence: bad data causes 14% of rework / $88.69 billion globally; removing the input cause removes the output errors.)*
2. **Ground AI tools in curated project documents rather than open chatbots.** Purpose-built tools grounded in a vetted corpus hallucinate **17–33%** of the time on verifiable professional queries, versus **58–88%** for general-purpose models — grounding roughly halves to two-thirds the error rate. The residual 17–33% is why step 3 exists. *(Direct outcome evidence, measured error rates.)*
3. **Make human verification procedural for technical and safety-relevant outputs.** In the BCG field experiment, consultants using AI on a task outside its capability were **19 percentage points** less likely to reach the correct answer, and the harm concentrated among those who accepted output with too little scrutiny. Microsoft/CMU found the staff who trust AI most scrutinise it least (β = −0.69) — verification must be a protocol, not a personal trait. *(Direct outcome evidence for the failure mechanism; the protocol itself is causal-chain.)*

### Risk 2 — lack of AI governance policies

1. **Adopt a formal AI strategy and governance framework with a named owner.** Organisations with a formal AI strategy report an **80%** AI adoption success rate versus **37%** without (Writer). Thomson Reuters found strategy-holders **3.5x** as likely to see critical AI benefits and **~2x** as likely to already be seeing AI-driven revenue growth — yet only **22%** have a visible strategy. Mature governance also enables rather than slows adoption: organisations with mature programmes are **2x** as likely to adopt agentic AI successfully (CSA). *(Survey-correlation evidence; consistent across three independent samples.)*
2. **Keep an AI inventory, project-level controls and an incident-response playbook.** **75%** of organisations have an AI usage policy but only **36%** a formal governance framework, **59%** a governance role, **54%** an incident playbook and **48%** production monitoring (Pacific AI) — the sector-level equivalents are worse: only **19–20%** have any documented AI policy (RIBA; Pinsent Masons), and **37%** lack project controls where AI is used for design and construction. Of breached organisations with AI policies, only **34%** audit for unsanctioned AI (IBM). *(Causal-chain evidence: governance absence is a named failure cause in Gartner and MIT NANDA analyses.)*
3. **Enforce AI access controls and integrate governance with security.** Of organisations breached through AI models or applications, **97%** lacked AI access controls (IBM); high shadow-AI usage added about **$670,000** to breach costs. Organisations using security AI and automation extensively saved an average **$1.9 million** and shortened breach lifecycles by **80 days**. But a published policy alone changes little — only **18.5%** of employees are even aware their company has one. *(Direct outcome evidence from the breach-cost data; the awareness finding is survey evidence.)*

### Risk 3 — lack of staff knowledge on using and supervising AI tools

1. **Role-based AI training rather than generic awareness.** Only **39%** of AI users have received employer training, but the users who get the most value — "power users" saving 30+ minutes daily — are **37%** more likely to have employer-provided AI learning programmes, with gains from prompt training (**+37%**), role-specific training (**+35%**) and use-case training (**+32%**) (Microsoft/LinkedIn, 31,000 respondents). Employers expect AI-skilled staff to lift productivity **39–47%**, while only **14%** of workers have advanced AI skills and three-quarters of employers cannot hire them — training the existing workforce is the only scalable route (AWS/Access Partnership). In construction specifically, NCCER found firms providing craft training hit productivity estimates about **two-thirds of the time versus just over 50%** for non-trainers, with less rework (**18.9% vs 21.8%**) — structured training measurably changes field outcomes in this sector. *(Training-outcome evidence: observational construction data plus cross-sector survey correlations.)*
2. **Provision sanctioned enterprise AI tools rather than banning personal ones.** When organisations provide sanctioned tools, unauthorised use drops **89%** (Unseen Security via CSA) — supply-side governance is the highest-leverage response, because **46%** of workers would keep using personal tools even if banned, and **82%** of data pasted into AI tools currently goes through personal accounts invisible to IT. *(Direct outcome evidence for the 89% drop, from vendor research; see caveats.)*
3. **Require verification-first supervision, sequenced human-first.** The BCG experiment shows the gain is real inside the frontier (**+12.2%** tasks, **~25%** faster, quality up more than **30%**, with the weakest consultants gaining most at **+43%**) and the harm is real outside it (**−19pp**); because the frontier is invisible from the task itself, supervision must assume either side. MIT's EEG study adds the sequencing rule: writers who drafted independently before consulting AI retained neural engagement and recall, while AI-first writers showed "cognitive debt" that persisted even when they later wrote unaided. *(Direct experimental evidence.)*

### Risk 4 — low or no ROI from adopting inappropriate tools

1. **Select use cases problem-first, with a defined, measurable outcome set before building.** Gartner's January 2026 analysis of hundreds of implementations found lack of business value is failure point No. 1, and that organisations which master these fundamentals — value and metrics defined first — turn pilots into production at **twice the rate**. MIT NANDA names the absence of a defined outcome before building as a root cause of the **95%** zero-return figure, and the average organisation already scraps **46%** of proofs-of-concept (S&P Global). *(Direct outcome evidence on what separates producers from abandoners.)*
2. **Buy or partner rather than build.** Purchasing tools from specialised vendors or partnering succeeds about **67%** of the time; internal builds succeed only about a third as often (MIT NANDA) — roughly a 3x success-rate difference for the same capability. *(Direct outcome evidence.)*
3. **Pilot with kill criteria, a dedicated implementation team and a savings baseline.** **38%** of US contractors are creating AI implementation teams and **40%** are allocating a dedicated AI budget (Dodge/CMiC). The payoff pattern is measurable in AEC: **68%** of AI-using firms report saving at least $50,000 and **46%** report saving 500–1,000 hours (Bluebeam), and **31%** of AI use cases reached full production in 2025, double the 2024 rate (ISG) — production discipline, not experimentation volume, is what moves. *(Direct outcome evidence for the savings; team structure is survey-correlation.)*

## What the evidence does and does not show

- Adoption statistics disagree because the questions differ: AGC-Sage counts intent to invest (61%), RICS counts regular use in live processes (<12%), RIBA counts any project use (74%). Quote the definition with the rate.
- Gartner's abandonment figures are analyst analyses of "hundreds of GenAI implementations" with no published method; the 60% AI-ready-data figure is a prediction, not an observation.
- MIT NANDA's 95% is based on roughly 300 public deployments plus executive interviews and measures P&L impact of generative AI pilots, cross-industry; it is not a construction-specific count, and "zero return" does not mean "no benefit" — productivity gains that do not reach the P&L still count as zero.
- FMI/PlanGrid rework dollar figures rest on the assumption that rework is 5% of construction spend; they are useful for scale, not measured national totals.
- Cyberhaven, LayerX and Corvus are security vendors whose telemetry is large-scale but whose interest in the problem is commercial; IBM's breach figures survey breached organisations, so they describe the breached population.
- The Microsoft/CMU and MIT Media Lab cognition studies are self-reported or small-sample (319 workers; 54 participants) and correlational; they indicate direction, not causal effect sizes.
- RICS's respondents skew UK (~48%); Bluebeam, Dodge and Autodesk survey largely their own customer bases; RIBA surveys chartered practices. None is a census of the global built environment.
- Most of what is missing is outcome data *for the built environment specifically* — the sector's own failure-rate evidence (RICS adoption maturity, RIBA productivity-without-fee-gains) proxies for it but does not measure P&L directly.
- The mitigation evidence has its own limits. The Writer, Thomson Reuters, Pacific AI and LinkedIn findings are survey self-reports: organisations that formalise strategy or training may differ in other ways too. IBM's breach data describes the breached population (600 organisations) — it measures what breached organisations lacked, not the effect of adopting controls. The 89% drop in unauthorised use from provisioning sanctioned tools comes from Unseen Security vendor research whose methodology is not published. NCCER's training comparison is observational and measures craft training, not AI training. Dell'Acqua et al. is the strongest direct evidence — randomised and preregistered — but studies consultants, not construction professionals, with GPT-4-era models. AWS's 39–47% productivity figures are employer expectations, not measured outcomes.
